\section*{Introduction}
In this report, we aim to explore housing affordability in Metro Vancouver, through analysis of the “Housing Affordability for New Homeowners in B.C., Census Metropolitan Areas Quarterly Data 2025 Q3” dataset obtained from the B.C. Data Catalogue. The complete dataset contains data from all major cities in BC, gathered quarterly. The data was gathered to improve affordability across BC. This data was collected through a combined effort by Statistics Canada, Bank of Canada and BC Assessment, and compiled by BC Stats. They used informational sources such as CRA tax returns, CPI surveys, and property information gathered via BC assessments. Source: \url{https://catalogue.data.gov.bc.ca/dataset/b-c-housing-affordability/resource/a71e6838-cda1-4acb-adfb-8515c4f9fec9} \\ \\
As students currently struggling with affordability in Metro Vancouver, we wanted to investigate housing affordability trends in this area and its association to variables such as housing prices, the prime rate, and family income. Specifically, we want to know how interest rates and median family income affect median housing price, after accounting for housing type and number of resales. This will help us understand the key factors driving market price, and potentially derive our own conclusions on affordability trends.

To finalize our predictive model, we compared four candidate subsets using Adjusted $R^2$ and Mallows' $C_p$ criteria. Our objective was to select the most parsimonious model, i.e., the simplest version that maintains high predictive accuracy without introducing bias.\
As shown in the comparison table, the \textbf{Base Model} (including Housing Type, Income, and Prime Rate) achieved an \textbf{Adjusted $R^2$ of 0.9539}. Adding number of resales (\texttt{resales}) and seasonality (\texttt{quarter}) only increased the $R^2$ by a negligible \textbf{0.0001}, suggesting these variables provide almost no additional explanatory power.\
Furthermore, the \textbf{Mallows' $C_p$} for the Base Model was \textbf{6.83}, which is close to the value we expect (7). This indicates that the model is unbiased and follows the \textbf{Principle of Parsimony}. In contrast, the more complex models increased complexity without a meaningful gain in fit. \
Therefore, the Base Model was selected as the most robust and efficient representation of housing prices in Metro Vancouver.

\section*{Discussion}

Using linear regression we were able to derive the following model: \\

$log(median\_housing\_price) = 11.3 - 0.340\,I\{Condo\} + 0.452\,I\{Single\_Detached\} - 0.104\,I\{Townhouse\} + 0.278(income\_10k) - 0.0283(prime\_interest\_rate)$ \\

The fitted model observes that a higher median family income is associated with higher median housing prices, whereas higher prime interest rates are associated with lower median housing prices. Furthermore, we notice a difference in price among the different housing types. We expect that single-detached homes have the highest expected prices, followed by townhouses, `All housing types', and then condos. We log-transformed median housing price to help stabilize the variance and make linear regression more appropriate. However, because of that choice, the coefficients above correspond to multiplicative changes in median housing price rather than 1:1 dollar changes. All of the coefficients proved to be significant, with p-values lower than the threshold for a 95\% confidence level. Additionally, the 95\% confidence interval of our coefficients did not cross zero. Both of these facts can be observed in Table 7. Meaning we can be fairly confident in our results. \\

Our model is able to explain roughly 95.4\% of the variation in log median housing prices. The appropriateness of our model is however up to debate. The plots in figure 4, namely the residual-fitted plot shows a consistent spread and QQ-plot, show a relatively consistent spread supporting the assumption of constant variance. This assumption is further supported by residual-interest rate covariate plot in figure 5. All of these results support the use of linear regression as an appropriate model for this dataset. \\

On the other hand, the residual-median family income plot showed concerning trends at the extreme left and right ends suggesting heteroscedastic variance. Additionally, the sequential residual plot shows a clear ``wave-like'' pattern suggesting a lack of independence among sequential observations. These two facts suggest that the errors we found are not fully independent. This makes some sense, as our current model does not directly factor in time. The housing markets are affected by time and economic/political environment. These markets tend to go up and down. Some specific examples we can consider are major economic events such as the 2008 American recession, or the recent downturn in economic performance. There appears to be an almost cyclical up then down cycle of housing prices that a simple linear model cannot account for. Furthermore, we were limited in what covariates we could include in our model due to concerns of multicollinearity across the different variables. Year was removed because of its high collinearity with other predictors such as median income. If we wanted to include year we would have to remove median income, and vice-versa, if we wanted a stable model. Another issue with our model is that it does not account for resales, this is a conscious choice as adding resales as a parameter in our model did not significantly change its accuracy. However, we then must ask the question if we fully addressed our original research question which asks about resale effects. \\

In terms of the data itself, the study is observational - meaning we can make at most associative determinations from our model. Additionally, the study uses aggregate quarterly measures meaning variables such as median housing price and median family income are the same across different rows in the same year/quarter. Household-level variation cannot be determined, and figuring out the skew of housing prices within the same quarter seems difficult. \\

In the future, we would prefer to choose a model more focused on time-series data and the non-linear relations between housing price and our covariates. It would also be helpful to have in this new non-linear model other market related variables un-related to housing. For example, it may be interesting to see how inflation, or recession indicators affect the median housing price. \\

In summary, the model is useful for capturing some relations between median housing price, family income, and interest rates, but it fails to capture hidden temporal relations and the nature of the data may cause slight violations of constant variance and independence assumptions backing the linear regression model.
